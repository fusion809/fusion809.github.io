No rolling resistance and air resistance losses

In the event of there being no non-conservative forces like rolling resistance and air resistance acting on the trike, the total energy of the system is given by:

E=T+V\begin{aligned} E &= T + V \end{aligned}E​=T+V​

Where TTT is kinetic energy (in joules) and VVV is potential energy (also in joules). In turn, they are defined as:

T=mv22\begin{align} T=\dfrac{mv^2}{2} \tag{1} \end{align}T=2mv2​​(1)​

and

V=mgh(s).\begin{align} V=mgh(s). \tag{2} \end{align}V=mgh(s).​(2)​

Where: vvv is velocity; mmm is the mass of the tricycle (in kilograms), including the mass of anything on it (e.g. its rider); ggg is gravitational acceleration (in metres per second squared (m⋅s−2\mathrm{m}\cdot \mathrm{s}^{-2}m⋅s−2)); and h(s)h(s)h(s) is the height (in mmm) of the base of the tricycle above sea level. The mghmghmgh value for VVV assumes constant gravitational acceleration.

Alternatively, if gravity is not constant:

V=GMmrbase−GMmrsummit=GMm(rbase−1−rsummit−1).\begin{aligned} V &= \dfrac{GMm}{r_{\mathrm{base}}} - \dfrac{GMm}{r_{\mathrm{summit}}}\\ &=GMm\left(r_{\mathrm{base}}^{-1}-r_{\mathrm{summit}}^{-1}\right). \end{aligned}V​=rbase​GMm​−rsummit​GMm​=GMm(rbase−1​−rsummit−1​).​

Where GGG is the gravitational constant, MMM is the mass of the Earth and rsummitr_{\mathrm{summit}}rsummit​ and rbaser_{\mathrm{base}}rbase​ are the distances of the summit and base of the hill/mountain from the centre of the Earth.

If we are at the top of the hill and the tricycle is stationary, then E=VE=VE=V. So, when the trike moves downhill, TTT will eventually equal VVV at the bottom of the hill or mountain. Hence to determine the maximum of vvv we merely need to solve:

mv22=mgh.\begin{aligned} \dfrac{mv^2}{2} &= mgh. \end{aligned}2mv2​​=mgh.​

Multiplying both sides by 2m\dfrac{2}{m}m2​ yields:

v2=2gh.\begin{aligned} v^2 &= 2gh. \end{aligned}v2​=2gh.​

Whereas square rooting both sides yields:

v=2gh.\begin{align} v &= \sqrt{2gh}. \tag{3} \end{align}v​=2gh​.​(3)​

We deliberately ignored the negative solution for vvv as we have defined sss to be increasing as we go downhill and hence velocity is positive in this direction. This also means that so long as motion is in the direction of downhill, which it always will be when it is moving solely under the force of gravity, velocity will always be positive.

Or, if gravity cannot be assumed constant:

mv22=GMm(rbase−1−rsummit−1)v2=2GM(rbase−1−rsummit−1).\begin{aligned} \dfrac{mv^2}{2} &= GMm\left(r_{\mathrm{base}}^{-1}-r_{\mathrm{summit}}^{-1}\right) \\ v^2 &= 2GM \left(r_{\mathrm{base}}^{-1}-r_{\mathrm{summit}}^{-1}\right). \end{aligned}2mv2​v2​=GMm(rbase−1​−rsummit−1​)=2GM(rbase−1​−rsummit−1​).​

Square rooting both sides yields:

v=2GM(rbase−1−rsummit−1).\begin{align} v &= \sqrt{2GM\left(r_{\mathrm{base}}^{-1}-r_{\mathrm{summit}}^{-1}\right)}. \tag{4} \end{align}v​=2GM(rbase−1​−rsummit−1​)​.​(4)​

In Equation (4), we deliberately ignored the negative possibility as the velocity as we only care about the magnitude of the velocity not its direction. Keeping in mind that the tallest point of Castle Hill is not reachable by road, so maximum altitude would be approximately somewhere in the vicinity of 265.9 metres above sea level — at least based on what Google Fit says of my altitude when I journey there. According to Equations (3) and (4), the maximum velocity of a trike travelling down Castle Hill would be approximately 260 kilometres per hour (kph).

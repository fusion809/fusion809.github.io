Partial differentiation

Partial derivatives are just a straightforward extension of derivatives as you understand them already. The key difference is that they are derivatives of multivariable functions instead of single-variable functions. Further, when you differentiate with respect to one variable, all other variables are kept constant. If you would like to build a graphical image of what the partial derivative is, well recall that the derivative of a single-variable function is just the gradient of that function. Consequently, it is also the gradient of the tangent line to that function. The partial derivative is similar to that. As when we have a function with more than a single independent variable, we have the freedom to choose which variable or variables we wish to determine our function's gradient with respect to.

If you want to visualize this, imagine a surface defined by z=f(x,y)z=f(x,y)z=f(x,y) for example. Because a tangent line is two dimensional and we're in three dimensional space, there can be more than one tangent line to a given point. As when constructing the tangent line, we must answer a question: in which direction do we want our line to be tangential to our surface? It could be tangential in the xxx direction or the yyy direction, or even in any arbitrary direction in the xxx-yyy plane. If we want the tangent line in the xxx direction, we will need the partial derivative with respect to xxx to get its gradient, whereas if we want the tangent line in the yyy direction, we will need the partial derivative with respect to yyy to get its gradient. More generally, for an arbitrary direction in the xxx-yyy plane we would use the directional derivative as it is called.

Say fff is a function of two variables — xxx and yyy. Then its partial derivative with respect to xxx is formally defined as:

∂f∂x=lim⁡h→0f(x+h,y)−f(x,y)h.\begin{aligned} \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x} &= \lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \dfrac{f(x+h, y)-f(x, y)}{h}. \end{aligned}∂x∂f​​=h→0lim​hf(x+h,y)−f(x,y)​.​

This is analogous to the first principles definition of the derivative you would have learned for standard derivatives. Namely, if we are differentiating f(x)f(x)f(x):

dfdx=lim⁡h→0f(x+h)−f(x)h.\begin{aligned} \dfrac{df}{dx} &= \lim_{h\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}. \end{aligned}dxdf​​=h→0lim​hf(x+h)−f(x)​.​

The only difference is that now we also have a variable yyy that our function f(x,y)f(x,y)f(x,y) depends on, but is kept constant when differentiating with respect to xxx. Say f(x,y)=2x2yf(x,y)=2x^2yf(x,y)=2x2y. Then ∂f∂x\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}∂x∂f​ would be:

∂f∂x=lim⁡h→02(x+h)2y−2x2yh.\begin{aligned} \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x} &= \lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \dfrac{2(x+h)^2y - 2x^2y}{h}. \end{aligned}∂x∂f​​=h→0lim​h2(x+h)2y−2x2y​.​

Did you notice that 2y2y2y is a common factor of both terms in our numerator? Let us take it out of our limit altogether, as it is effectively a constant (it doesn't depend on hhh, after all).

∂f∂x=2ylim⁡h→0(x+h)2−x2h\begin{aligned} \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x} &= 2y\lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \dfrac{(x+h)^2-x^2}{h} \end{aligned}∂x∂f​​=2yh→0lim​h(x+h)2−x2​​

Using the binomial theorem, we know that

(x+h)2=x2+2xh+h2.\begin{aligned} (x+h)^2 &= x^2 + 2xh + h^2. \end{aligned}(x+h)2​=x2+2xh+h2.​

Hence:

∂f∂x=2ylim⁡h→0x2+2xh+h2−x2h=2ylim⁡h→02xh+h2h=2ylim⁡h→02x+h=2y⋅2x=4xy.\begin{aligned} \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x} &= 2y\lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \dfrac{x^2+2xh + h^2-x^2}{h} \\ &= 2y\lim_{h\rightarrow 0} \dfrac{2xh + h^2}{h} \\ &= 2y\lim_{h\rightarrow 0} 2x + h \\ &= 2y \cdot 2x \\ &= 4xy. \end{aligned}∂x∂f​​=2yh→0lim​hx2+2xh+h2−x2​=2yh→0lim​h2xh+h2​=2yh→0lim​2x+h=2y⋅2x=4xy.​

Okay, so that is the long way of finding out partial derivatives. Fortunately, there is also a faster way. Which is that we apply normal differentiation rules, but any variable we are not differentiating with respect to is treated like it's a constant. So for f(x,y)=2x2yf(x,y)=2x^2yf(x,y)=2x2y we would treat the 2y2y2y as just a constant number and only differentiate the x2x^2x2. Applying the power rule, we see that ddxx2=2x\dfrac{d}{dx}x^2 = 2xdxd​x2=2x and hence:

∂f∂x=2y⋅2x=4xy.\begin{aligned} \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x} &= 2y \cdot 2x \\ &= 4xy. \end{aligned}∂x∂f​​=2y⋅2x=4xy.​

Heat equation example

One of the simplest partial differential equations is the heat equation. In its simplest form it is:

∂u∂t=α∂2u∂x2\begin{align} \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t} &= \alpha \dfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} \tag{1} \end{align}∂t∂u​​=α∂x2∂2u​​(1)​

Where u(x,t)u(x,t)u(x,t) is a function describing the temperature of the one-dimensional object (only one dimensional as the above heat equation only has a single spatial dimension, if we want more spatial dimensions we'll have to add second partial derivatives with respect to those other dimensions, too) we're modelling and α\alphaα is some constant. I know this equation may look confusing at first glance, but it is actually saying something rather intuitive to you.

Do you remember from your maths class that the second derivative of a function is its concavity? So if the second derivative is negative, that means our function is concave down — it is leading to some sort of maximum. Whereas if it is positive, the function is concave up, which means it is going up from some minimum. We have the second derivative with respect to space on the right-hand side of Equation (1), so it essentially returns positive if we have a localized colder area and neighbouring hotter points. Whereas it returns negative if we have a really hot point surrounded by cooler points. The left-hand side of Equation (1), however, represents the rate of change of our function in time.

Equation (1) therefore essentially says that if we are in a region where there's a really cold point surrounded by hotter points, the temperature will increase in the short-term for that colder point. Whereas the equation says that the temperature will decrease in a hotter region surrounded by colder points. If you've ever brought something hot out of the oven, or put something of room temperature into the fridge or freezer, this should make intuitive sense to you.

Lagrangian mechanics

Lagrangian mechanics is one of three major formulations of classical mechanics with the others being Newtonian mechanics and Hamiltonian mechanics. Classical mechanics as a whole is a branch of physics most applicable to the situations that humans most often encounter, as it does not account for quantum randomness (hence cannot be applied to very small scales), nor does it account for relativistic effects (which are most relevant when dealing with strong gravitational fields or velocities close to the speed of light). In Lagrangian mechanics, we define a quantity known as the Lagrangian of our physical system:

L=T−V.\begin{aligned} L &= T - V. \end{aligned}L​=T−V.​

Where TTT is kinetic energy and VVV is potential energy of our entire physical system. It is important to note that only conservative forces contribute to VVV. Lagrangian mechanics can account for non-conservative (i.e. dissipative) forces, too, but we will get to how one handles them later.

The distinction between conservative and non-conservative forces is whether they change the net energy of your system. For instance, gravity doesn't because any increase in kinetic energy it causes is balanced by a corresponding reduction in potential energy. Likewise, any loss of kinetic energy caused by gravity is balanced by an increase in potential energy. Consequently, it is a conservative force. Air resistance, however, is a non-conservative force because the losses it causes in kinetic energy are not balanced by increases in potential energy.

Euler-Lagrange equations

When there are no non-conservative forces, the equations of motion can be derived by first determining our system's Lagrangian and then applying a set of partial differential equations known as the Euler-Lagrange equations to this Lagrangian. The Euler-Lagrange equations are:

ddt∂L∂q˙i−∂L∂qi=0,i∈{1,2,3,...,n}.\begin{align} \hspace{4cm}\dfrac{d}{dt} \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i} - \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} &= 0, \hspace{2cm} i\in \{1,2,3,...,n\}. \tag{2} \end{align}dtd​∂q˙​i​∂L​−∂qi​∂L​​=0,i∈{1,2,3,...,n}.​(2)​

qiq_iqi​ are the nnn degrees of freedom of our problem and q˙i\dot{q}_iq˙​i​ are their time derivatives. The ddt\dfrac{d}{dt}dtd​ is not a partial derivative and this is purposeful, as when we differentiate ∂L∂q˙i\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i}∂q˙​i​∂L​ with respect to ttt, we treat qiq_iqi​ and q˙i\dot{q}_iq˙​i​ as functions of ttt and wherever they appear in ∂L∂q˙i\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i}∂q˙​i​∂L​ we differentiate them against ttt.

Next, I should explain what "degrees of freedom" are. They are also known as generalized coordinates and they are essentially our variables of interest. For simpler problems, the degrees of freedom in our problem are often the spatial dimensions of our problem. For instance, if we were to study an object in free fall and we were to try to simplify our problem as much as possible and not consider any movement in the horizontal directions then nnn could be 1 and q1q_1q1​ would just be the height of the object above ground level.

Likewise, when we study the simple pendulum — which is a problem in which we have an idealized rigid pendulum whose rod has zero mass, zero friction and for which there is absolutely no air resistance — typically we only consider a single degree of freedom, which is the angle our pendulum's rod makes with either the negative yyy axis or the positive xxx axis, depending on the preferences of whoever is setting up the problem.

Derivation of Equations (2)

The derivation of Equations (2) requires knowledge of a field of mathematics known as the calculus of variations as well as a physics principle known as the stationary action principle. If you are interested in learning more, I'd suggest you check out Veritasium's video on this topic (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q10_srZ-pbs). In short, I will say that there's a quantity known as the action, which is denoted as SSS, that is defined as:

S=∫t0tfL(qi,q˙i,t)dt.\begin{align} S &= \int_{t_0}^{t_f} L(q_i, \dot{q}_i, t) dt. \tag{3} \end{align}S​=∫t0​tf​​L(qi​,q˙​i​,t)dt.​(3)​

One interesting property of the action is that nature consistently sets it to its stationary point, and this is regardless of context. Regardless of whether we're examining the motion of planets with Newton's second law, or studying the behaviour of black holes with general relativity, or checking out the behaviour of subatomic particles, there is always an action that is at its stationary point. Traditionally, this stationary point is typically thought of as a minimum (hence why this is also known as the principle of least action), but strictly speaking it is not always a minimum. When you use the calculus of variations to find the stationary point of Equation (3), Equations (2) pop out.

Hamiltonian mechanics

In Hamiltonian mechanics, we define the Hamiltonian as:

H=T+V.\begin{align} H &= T + V. \tag{10} \end{align}H​=T+V.​(10)​

Which is the total energy of our system. Another difference from Lagrangian mechanics is that we define HHH in terms of pip_ipi​ and qiq_iqi​ instead of qiq_iqi​ and q˙i\dot{q}_iq˙​i​. The equations of motion with no non-conservative forces are:

This creates a new kind of space that we can visualize our solution on that has dimensions qiq_iqi​ and pip_ipi​. This type of space is known as phase space.

H=ml22(pθml2)2+mglsin⁡θ=ml22pθ2m2l4+mglsin⁡θ.\begin{aligned} H &= \dfrac{ml^2}{2} \left( \dfrac{p_{\theta}}{ml^2} \right)^2 + mgl\sin{\theta} \\ &= \dfrac{ml^2}{2} \dfrac{p_{\theta}^2}{m^2l^4} + mgl\sin{\theta}. \end{aligned}H​=2ml2​(ml2pθ​​)2+mglsinθ=2ml2​m2l4pθ2​​+mglsinθ.​

So our final Hamiltonian is:

H=pθ22ml2+mglsin⁡θ.\begin{align} H &= \dfrac{p_{\theta}^2}{2ml^2} + mgl\sin{\theta}. \tag{17} \end{align}H​=2ml2pθ2​​+mglsinθ.​(17)​

Hence the equations of motion are:

p˙θ=−∂H∂θ=−mglcos⁡θ\begin{align} \dot{p}_{\theta} &= -\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial \theta} \notag \\ &= - mgl\cos{\theta} \tag{18} \end{align}p˙​θ​​=−∂θ∂H​=−mglcosθ​(18)​

and

θ˙=∂H∂pθ=pθml2.\begin{align} \dot{\theta} &= \dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_{\theta}} \notag \\ &= \dfrac{p_{\theta}}{ml^2}. \tag{19} \end{align}θ˙​=∂pθ​∂H​=ml2pθ​​.​(19)​

As we can see, Hamiltonian mechanics yields a series of first-order ordinary differential equations (ODEs) instead of second-order ODEs. Which is important because Lagrangian mechanics and Newtonian mechanics both yield second-order ODE systems. That being said, if you rewrite Equation (19) to make pθp_{\theta}pθ​ its subject and substitute it into Equation (18) and rearrange it to make θ¨\ddot{\theta}θ¨ the subject, you will obtain Equation (9).

Poisson brackets

Poisson brackets provide us an elegant and powerful way to rewrite Hamilton's equations. If we have nnn degrees of freedom and two functions fff and ggg that depend on pip_ipi​ and qiq_iqi​:[1]

{f,g}=∑i=1n(∂f∂qi∂g∂pi−∂f∂pi∂g∂qi).\begin{align} \{f, g\} &= \sum_{i=1}^n \left( \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_i} \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_i} - \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_i} \right). \tag{20} \end{align}{f,g}​=i=1∑n​(∂qi​∂f​∂pi​∂g​−∂pi​∂f​∂qi​∂g​).​(20)​

Consequently:

q˙k={qk,H}p˙k=−{pk,H}.\begin{align} \dot{q}_k &= \{q_k, H\} \tag{21} \\ \dot{p}_k &= -\{p_k, H\}. \tag{22} \end{align}q˙​k​p˙​k​​={qk​,H}=−{pk​,H}.​(21)(22)​

We can establish Equations (21) with

{qk,H}=∑i=1n(∂qk∂qi∂H∂pi−∂qk∂pi∂H∂qi).\begin{align} \{q_k, H\} &= \sum_{i=1}^n \left(\dfrac{\partial q_k}{\partial q_i}\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} - \dfrac{\partial q_k}{\partial p_i}\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\right). \tag{23} \end{align}{qk​,H}​=i=1∑n​(∂qi​∂qk​​∂pi​∂H​−∂pi​∂qk​​∂qi​∂H​).​(23)​

∂qk∂qi=δik\dfrac{\partial q_k}{\partial q_i}=\delta_{ik}∂qi​∂qk​​=δik​, where δik\delta_{ik}δik​ is the Kronecker delta function which is is equal to one if i=ki=ki=k and zero otherwise. This equation holds because we assume our degrees of freedom or coordinates are independent of each other. For instance, yyy is independent of xxx in Cartesian coordinates and θ\thetaθ is independent of rrr in polar coordinates. As for ∂qk∂pi\dfrac{\partial q_k}{\partial p_i}∂pi​∂qk​​ it is zero. Hence Equation (25) becomes:

{qk,H}=∑i=1nδik∂H∂pi.\begin{align} \{q_k, H\} &= \sum_{i=1}^n \delta_{ik} \dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}. \tag{24} \end{align}{qk​,H}​=i=1∑n​δik​∂pi​∂H​.​(24)​

Summing over iii yields only one term, thanks to the Kronecker delta, namely the i=ki=ki=k term, as all other terms have a zero multiplier:

{qk,H}=∂H∂pk.\begin{align} \{q_k, H\} &= \dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_k}. \tag{25} \end{align}{qk​,H}​=∂pk​∂H​.​(25)​

Thanks to Hamilton's equations, we know the right-hand side of Equation (25) is equal to q˙k\dot{q}_kq˙​k​.

We can establish Equations (22) with:

{pk,H}=∑i=1n(∂pk∂qi∂H∂pi−∂pk∂pi∂H∂qi)\begin{align} \{p_k, H\} &= \sum_{i=1}^n \left(\dfrac{\partial p_k}{\partial q_i}\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} - \dfrac{\partial p_k}{\partial p_i}\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\right) \tag{26} \end{align}{pk​,H}​=i=1∑n​(∂qi​∂pk​​∂pi​∂H​−∂pi​∂pk​​∂qi​∂H​)​(26)​

∂pk∂qi\dfrac{\partial p_k}{\partial q_i}∂qi​∂pk​​ is zero and ∂pk∂pi\dfrac{\partial p_k}{\partial p_i}∂pi​∂pk​​ is δik\delta_{ik}δik​. Hence:

{pk,H}=∑i=1n(−δik∂H∂qi)=−∂H∂qk.\begin{align} \{p_k, H\} &= \sum_{i=1}^n \left(-\delta_{ik} \dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\right) \tag{27} \\ &= -\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q_k}. \tag{28} \end{align}{pk​,H}​=i=1∑n​(−δik​∂qi​∂H​)=−∂qk​∂H​.​(27)(28)​

According to Hamilton's equations, the right-hand side of Equations (28) should equal p˙k\dot{p}_kp˙​k​.

Poisson brackets also have several interesting properties, such as antisymmetry:

{A,B}=−{B,A}.\begin{align} \{A, B\} &= -\{B, A\}. \tag{29} \end{align}{A,B}​=−{B,A}.​(29)​

Further:

{qk,pk}=∑i=1n(∂qk∂qi∂pk∂pi−∂qk∂pi∂pk∂qi)=∑i=1nδikδik=1{qj,qk}=∑i=1n(∂qj∂qi∂qk∂pi−∂qj∂pi∂qk∂qi)=0{pj,pk}=∑i=1n(∂pj∂qi∂pk∂pi−∂pj∂pi∂pk∂qi)=0.\begin{align} \{q_k, p_k\} &= \sum_{i=1}^n \left( \dfrac{\partial q_k}{\partial q_i} \dfrac{\partial p_k}{\partial p_i} - \dfrac{\partial q_k}{\partial p_i} \dfrac{\partial p_k}{\partial q_i} \right) \notag \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n \delta_{ik}\delta_{ik} \notag \\ &= 1 \tag{30} \\ \{q_j, q_k\} &= \sum_{i=1}^n \left( \dfrac{\partial q_j}{\partial q_i} \dfrac{\partial q_k}{\partial p_i} - \dfrac{\partial q_j}{\partial p_i} \dfrac{\partial q_k}{\partial q_i} \right) \notag \\ &= 0 \tag{31} \\ \{p_j, p_k\} &= \sum_{i=1}^n \left(\dfrac{\partial p_j}{\partial q_i}\dfrac{\partial p_k}{\partial p_i} - \dfrac{\partial p_j}{\partial p_i}\dfrac{\partial p_k}{\partial q_i}\right) \notag \\ &= 0. \tag{32} \end{align}{qk​,pk​}{qj​,qk​}{pj​,pk​}​=i=1∑n​(∂qi​∂qk​​∂pi​∂pk​​−∂pi​∂qk​​∂qi​∂pk​​)=i=1∑n​δik​δik​=1=i=1∑n​(∂qi​∂qj​​∂pi​∂qk​​−∂pi​∂qj​​∂qi​∂qk​​)=0=i=1∑n​(∂qi​∂pj​​∂pi​∂pk​​−∂pi​∂pj​​∂qi​∂pk​​)=0.​(30)(31)(32)​

The quality of two functions, say fff and ggg, having a Poisson bracket that equals zero is phrased as fff and ggg commuting under the Poisson bracket.

If a function f(qi,pi)f(q_i, p_i)f(qi​,pi​) is not explicitly dependent on ttt and only depends on ttt indirectly through qiq_iqi​ and pip_ipi​, then, via the chain rule:

dfdt=∑i=1n∂f∂qidqidt+∂f∂pidpidt.\begin{align} \dfrac{df}{dt} &= \sum_{i=1}^n \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\dfrac{d q_i}{dt} + \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\dfrac{dp_i}{dt}. \tag{33} \end{align}dtdf​​=i=1∑n​∂qi​∂f​dtdqi​​+∂pi​∂f​dtdpi​​.​(33)​

We can use Hamilton's equations to replace dqidt\dfrac{dq_i}{dt}dtdqi​​ and dpidt\dfrac{dp_i}{dt}dtdpi​​:

dfdt=∑i=1n∂f∂qi∂H∂pi−∂f∂pi∂H∂qi.\begin{align} \dfrac{df}{dt} &= \sum_{i=1}^n \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} - \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}. \tag{34} \end{align}dtdf​​=i=1∑n​∂qi​∂f​∂pi​∂H​−∂pi​∂f​∂qi​∂H​.​(34)​

Interestingly, the right-hand side is equal to the Poisson bracket of fff with HHH, in other words:

dfdt={f,H}.\begin{align} \dfrac{df}{dt} &= \{f, H\}. \tag{35} \end{align}dtdf​​={f,H}.​(35)​

Hence commuting with the Hamiltonian under the Poisson bracket is a sufficient condition for a quantity that does not explicitly depend on time to have a zero time derivative and hence be conserved.

Noether's theorem

Noether's theorem essentially states that for every continuous symmetry in the Lagrangian, there is a corresponding conservation law. For instance, time translation symmetry of the Lagrangian yields the conservation of energy. To show this, we will assume our Lagrangian does not explicitly depend on ttt. That is, ttt itself does not appear directly in our Lagrangian. The only time dependence LLL has on ttt is through the dependence of qiq_iqi​ and q˙i\dot{q}_iq˙​i​ on time. If the Lagrangian did explicitly depend on time, we would expect it not to have time translation symmetry. Say we increase time according to the transformation: t→t+ϵt\rightarrow t+\epsilont→t+ϵ.

L(qi(t+ϵ),q˙i(t+ϵ))≈L(qi(t),q˙i(t))+dLdtϵ=L(qi(t),q˙i(t))+∑i=1n∂L∂qidqidtϵ+∂L∂q˙idq˙idtϵ..\begin{align} L(q_i(t+\epsilon), \dot{q}_i(t+\epsilon)) &\approx L(q_i(t), \dot{q}_i(t)) + \dfrac{dL}{dt} \epsilon \tag{36} \\ &= L(q_i(t), \dot{q}_i(t)) + \sum_{i=1}^n\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} \dfrac{dq_i}{dt} \epsilon + \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i} \dfrac{d\dot{q}_i}{dt} \epsilon .. \tag{37} \end{align}L(qi​(t+ϵ),q˙​i​(t+ϵ))​≈L(qi​(t),q˙​i​(t))+dtdL​ϵ=L(qi​(t),q˙​i​(t))+i=1∑n​∂qi​∂L​dtdqi​​ϵ+∂q˙​i​∂L​dtdq˙​i​​ϵ..​(36)(37)​

Subtracting L(qi(t),q˙i(t))L(q_i(t), \dot{q}_i(t))L(qi​(t),q˙​i​(t)) from both sides of Equation (37) yields:

L(qi(t+ϵ),q˙i(t+ϵ))−L(qi(t),q˙i(t))≈∑i=1n∂L∂qidqidtϵ+∂L∂q˙idq˙idtϵ.\begin{align} L(q_i(t+\epsilon), \dot{q}_i(t+\epsilon)) - L(q_i(t), \dot{q}_i(t)) &\approx \sum_{i=1}^n\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} \dfrac{dq_i}{dt} \epsilon + \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i} \dfrac{d\dot{q}_i}{dt} \epsilon. \tag{38} \end{align}L(qi​(t+ϵ),q˙​i​(t+ϵ))−L(qi​(t),q˙​i​(t))​≈i=1∑n​∂qi​∂L​dtdqi​​ϵ+∂q˙​i​∂L​dtdq˙​i​​ϵ.​(38)​

We will call the left-hand side of Equation (38) ΔL\Delta LΔL, hence Equation (38) becomes:

ΔL≈∑i=1n∂L∂qidqidtϵ+∂L∂q˙idq˙idtϵ.\begin{align} \Delta L &\approx \sum_{i=1}^n \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} \dfrac{dq_i}{dt} \epsilon + \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i} \dfrac{d\dot{q}_i}{dt} \epsilon. \tag{39} \end{align}ΔL​≈i=1∑n​∂qi​∂L​dtdqi​​ϵ+∂q˙​i​∂L​dtdq˙​i​​ϵ.​(39)​

Rearranging Equations (2) yields that:

∂L∂qi=ddt(∂L∂q˙i).\begin{align} \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = \dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i}\right). \tag{40} \end{align}∂qi​∂L​=dtd​(∂q˙​i​∂L​).​(40)​

If you are wondering what about if we have non-conservative forces? Would that not mean Equations (40) would not hold as there would be a missing generalized force term? Yes and in that event the Lagrangian would indeed change in time and conservation of energy would not hold, as our system would be losing energy to those non-conservative forces.

Substituting Equations (40) into Equation (39) yields:

ΔL≈∑i=1nϵ(ddt∂L∂q˙iq˙i+∂L∂q˙iq¨i).\begin{align} \Delta L &\approx \sum_{i=1}^n \epsilon\left(\dfrac{d}{dt}\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i}\dot{q}_i + \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i}\ddot{q}_i\right). \tag{41} \end{align}ΔL​≈i=1∑n​ϵ(dtd​∂q˙​i​∂L​q˙​i​+∂q˙​i​∂L​q¨​i​).​(41)​

Substituting Equations (4) into Equation (41) yields:

ΔL≈ϵ∑i=1n(dpidtq˙i+piq¨i)=ϵ∑i=1n(p˙iq˙i+piq¨i).\begin{align} \Delta L &\approx \epsilon\sum_{i=1}^n \left(\dfrac{dp_i}{dt} \dot{q}_i + p_i \ddot{q}_i\right) \notag \\ &= \epsilon\sum_{i=1}^n \left(\dot{p}_i \dot{q}_i + p_i \ddot{q}_i\right). \tag{42} \end{align}ΔL​≈ϵi=1∑n​(dtdpi​​q˙​i​+pi​q¨​i​)=ϵi=1∑n​(p˙​i​q˙​i​+pi​q¨​i​).​(42)​

We can rewrite the bracketed expression on the right-hand side of Equation (42) using the product rule of differentiation as:

ΔL≈ϵ∑i=1nddt(piq˙i)=ϵddt∑i=1npiq˙i.\begin{aligned} \Delta L &\approx \epsilon\sum_{i=1}^n \dfrac{d}{dt}\left(p_i \dot{q}_i\right) \\ &= \epsilon\dfrac{d}{dt}\sum_{i=1}^n p_i \dot{q}_i. \end{aligned}ΔL​≈ϵi=1∑n​dtd​(pi​q˙​i​)=ϵdtd​i=1∑n​pi​q˙​i​.​

If LLL does not change in time, ΔL=0\Delta L=0ΔL=0, and hence:

ϵddt∑i=1npiq˙i=0.\begin{align} \epsilon \dfrac{d}{dt} \sum_{i=1}^n p_i \dot{q}_i &= 0. \tag{43} \end{align}ϵdtd​i=1∑n​pi​q˙​i​​=0.​(43)​

Dividing both sides of Equations (43) by ϵ\epsilonϵ yields:

ddt∑i=1npiq˙i=0.\begin{aligned} \dfrac{d}{dt} \sum_{i=1}^n p_i \dot{q}_i &= 0. \end{aligned}dtd​i=1∑n​pi​q˙​i​​=0.​

LLL does not change, so dLdt=0\dfrac{dL}{dt}=0dtdL​=0, too. Hence:

ddt(∑i=1npiq˙i−L)=0.\begin{align} \dfrac{d}{dt} \left(\sum_{i=1}^n p_i \dot{q}_i - L\right) &= 0. \tag{44} \end{align}dtd​(i=1∑n​pi​q˙​i​−L)​=0.​(44)​

Interestingly, ∑i=1npiq˙i=2T\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n p_i \dot{q}_i=2Ti=1∑n​pi​q˙​i​=2T, that is it is twice the kinetic energy of our system. Hence if we substitute Equation (1) into Equation (44), we obtain:

ddt(2T−(T−V))=0ddt(T+V)=0.\begin{align} \dfrac{d}{dt} \left(2T-(T-V)\right) &= 0 \notag \\ \dfrac{d}{dt} \left(T+V\right) &= 0. \tag{45} \end{align}dtd​(2T−(T−V))dtd​(T+V)​=0=0.​(45)​

Hence substituting Equation (10) into Equation (45) yields:

dHdt=0.\begin{align} \dfrac{dH}{dt} &= 0. \tag{46} \end{align}dtdH​​=0.​(46)​

In other words, we obtain that the Hamiltonian — the total energy of the system — does not change, hence it is conserved.

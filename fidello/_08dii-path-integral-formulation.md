### Path-integral formulation
One formulation of quantum mechanics is the path-integral formulation developed by Richard Feynmann. In it, we expect our particle or wave to explore all possible paths and we integrate (or sum) over all those paths in order to compute the probability amplitude of our particle. The exploration of all possible paths is a consequence of the randomness of quantum mechanics. Specifically, we compute the integral:

\begin{align}
K(\mathbf{x}_f, t_f; \mathbf{x}_0, t_0) &= \int_{\mathbf{x}(t_0)=\mathbf{x}_0}^{\mathbf{x}(t_f)=\mathbf{x}_f} e^{\frac{iS(\mathbf{x})}{\hbar}} \mathcal{D}\mathbf{x}.
\end{align}

Where $K$ is the propagator, which specifies the probability amplitude for travelling from $\mathbf{x}_0$ at time $t_0$ to $\mathbf{x}_f$ at time $t_f$. $S$ is the action of the particle, $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck's constant, $i$ is the imaginary number and $\mathcal{D}\mathbf{x}$ refers to the fact we are integrating over all possible paths. The probability amplitude is a quantity that, if you take the absolute value of, and square this quantity you get the probability of the particle travelling from $\mathbf{x}_0$ at time $t_0$ to $\mathbf{x}_f$ at time $t_f$. 

One major strength of the path-integral formulation of quantum mechanics is that it provides an explanation for how quantum mechanics gives rise to classical mechanics on larger scales. 

So, with the background information covered in [Prerequisite mathematics](#prerequisite_mathematics) in mind, we now know that $e^{\frac{iS}{\hbar}}$ is like a 2D unit vector as it points in particular direction in the $xy$ plane and has unit length. Specifically, it points in the direction $\dfrac{S}{\hbar}$ radians counterclockwise from the positive $x$ axis. Each integer multiple of $2\pi$, which corresponds to a complete revolution around the Cartesian plane, that is present in $\dfrac{S}{\hbar}$ makes no difference to its overall direction. What matters is what is left after subtracting these integer multiples of $2\pi$. 

Interestingly, when we integrate over all possible paths on classical mechanics scales we find that the $e^{\frac{iS}{\hbar}}$, for most paths, cancel each other out. To see why, we must first start with the fact that $\hbar$ is a very tiny number &mdash; it is approximately $1.055\times 10^{-34}\mathrm{J}\cdot \mathrm{s}$. Consequently, $\dfrac{S}{\hbar}$ is a massive number when we are analysing classical mechanical systems. As typically in these scenarios $S$ is $\gt0.01J\cdot s$. Consequently, even a relatively small difference in $S$, can lead to a majorly different direction for $e^{\frac{iS}{\hbar}}$.

This leads to the actions of nearby paths often being in opposite directions and cancelling each other when we calculate the path integral. The one exception is the path corresponding to the stationary action. Remember the equation $\delta S=0$ that we solve to find the path of our classical mechanical system? It says that a tiny change to the stationary action path should make no change (to first order) to the action and consequently, when we integrate $e^{\frac{iS}{\hbar}}$ for that path and its neighbours, the stationary action path term is the only one that survives. Consequently, in the classical limit our object only explores the path that extremizes the action, and as we have seen an extremized action leads to the equations of motion in classical mechanics.
